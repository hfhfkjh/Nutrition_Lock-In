## Part A: Core questions

### Q1: Phosphorus in the body

Where is phosphorus in the body and in what form? Compare its distribution with calcium.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: form**

As phosphate, phosphorus combined with oxygen.

**Step 2: where**

85% in the skeleton, 15% in soft tissues.

**Step 3: compare**

Calcium is 99% in bone. Phosphorus has a bigger share outside bone because it is in membranes, DNA and ATP in every cell.

From: §1

</details>

### Q2: Portions of phosphorus

An adult needs 550 mg phosphorus a day. A 200 ml glass of milk gives 247 mg and one tub of yoghurt gives 385 mg.

1. How many glasses of milk reach the RDA?
2. How many tubs of yoghurt?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: milk**

550 ÷ 247 = 2.2, so **3** glasses (round up, because 2 glasses give only 494 mg).

**Step 2: yoghurt**

550 ÷ 385 = 1.4, so **2** tubs.

### Q3: Phosphorus absorption and regulation

How much dietary phosphorus is absorbed? Describe the two routes. How is blood phosphorus made up and what happens in the kidney?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: amount**

55–70%.

**Step 2: routes**

Passive, concentration dependent (about 60–70% of absorption). Active, vitamin D dependent (about 30%).

**Step 3: blood**

13 mmol/L total: 12 mmol/L as phospholipids in red cells and lipoproteins, 1 mmol/L as inorganic phosphate.

**Step 4: kidney**

Filtration then resorption. Urine phosphorus equals dietary intake. Regulation is similar to calcium.

From: §3

</details>

### Q4: Phosphorus functions, deficiency, toxicity

List the structural and the other roles of phosphorus. What defines deficiency, and what does toxicity do?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: structural**

Hydroxyapatite in bone, phospholipids in membranes and lipid transport, DNA.

**Step 2: other**

pH buffering, ATP energy storage, phosphorylation to activate enzymes.

**Step 3: deficiency**

Rare. Hypophosphataemia below 0.3 mmol/L. Anorexia, anaemia, muscle weakness, bone pain, rickets, osteomalacia, infections, confusion.

**Step 4: toxicity**

Hyperphosphataemia. Disturbs hormonal control of calcium and calcifies tissues, particularly the kidney.

From: §4, §5

</details>

### Q5: Magnesium sources and absorption

Name four good magnesium foods and the EFSA AI. Describe the three absorption routes and how balance is maintained.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: foods and AI**

Wheat bran, almonds, spinach, raisin bran cereal. In general: whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, green leafy vegetables, mineral water.

AI: 300 mg women, 350 mg men.

**Step 2: absorption**

Passive paracellular (50–70%), solvent drag with water movement (10–20%), active transcellular (20–30%). Overall 20–70% absorbed in the small intestine.

**Step 3: balance**

By controlling intestinal absorption and urinary losses. The kidney reabsorbs 95%.

From: §6, §7

</details>

### Q6: Magnesium functions, deficiency, toxicity

What is magnesium's main job? Give three pathways. Describe deficiency and toxicity, including the UL and who it applies to.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: main job**

Cofactor for more than 300 enzymes.

**Step 2: pathways**

Glycolysis (glucose to pyruvate), β-oxidation of fatty acids, protein synthesis. Also ATP production, DNA synthesis, bone, muscle contraction, clotting, immune function.

**Step 3: deficiency**

Not a problem in healthy people. Comes from disease or genetic faults. Hypocalcaemia and hypocalciuria, abnormal neuromuscular function: cramps, tetany, arrhythmias, low potassium.

**Step 4: toxicity**

Not from food. EFSA UL 250 mg/day for non-food forms only. Nausea, diarrhoea, muscle weakness, low blood pressure, irregular heartbeat.

From: §8, §9

</details>
