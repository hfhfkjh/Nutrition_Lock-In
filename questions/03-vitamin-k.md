### Q2: Absorption and transport

How are K1 and K2 absorbed? How are both transported out of the liver?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: K1**

Absorbed in the small intestine in micelles. About 80% is absorbed.

**Step 2: K2**

Made by intestinal bacteria. Absorbed by passive diffusion from the ileum and colon.

**Step 3: transport**

Both forms are released from the liver in VLDL.

From: §2

</details>

### Q3: Carboxylation of glutamate

Explain what vitamin K does to proteins, and why that lets them work.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: the reaction**

Vitamin K is a cofactor for a vitamin K dependent enzyme. The enzyme adds a carboxyl group to glutamate residues on certain proteins.

**Step 2: the product**

The glutamate becomes γ-carboxyglutamate, Gla.

**Step 3: why it matters**

Gla can bind calcium. Plain glutamate cannot. Calcium binding is needed for the proteins to work in blood clotting and bone formation.

Prothrombin and several clotting proteins contain Gla. Without vitamin K they have no Gla and cannot work.

From: §3

</details>

### Q5: Clotting chain

Write out the blood clotting chain from inactive clotting factors to the clot. Say where vitamin K and calcium come in.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. Vitamin K and calcium are needed to activate the clotting factors.
2. Clotting factors convert prothrombin to thrombin.
3. Thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin.
4. Fibrin forms the clot.

Vitamin K acts at the start. It gives prothrombin and the clotting factors their Gla residues so they can bind calcium.

From: §3, §4

</details>

---

### Q6: Newborns, status and toxicity

a. Why are newborns at risk of vitamin K deficiency, and what is done about it?

b. Name two tests of vitamin K status.

c. What is known about vitamin K toxicity?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**a.** Newborns have limited stores, a sterile gut so no bacterial K2, and breast milk is low in vitamin K. Some develop vitamin K deficiency bleeding, VKDB, within 24 hours of birth. HSE 2004: every baby gets one dose of vitamin K at birth.

**b.** Prothrombin time (calcium + thromboplastin added to plasma). Plasma preprothrombin, which has little or no Gla.

**c.** No evidence of toxicity from natural forms and no upper limit. Toxicity is known only for the synthetic form. High doses reduce the effect of anticoagulants such as warfarin.

From: §6, §7

</details>

### M1: True or false

1. Menaquinone is made by bacteria in the gut.
2. Vitamin K is absorbed directly into the blood.
3. Gla residues allow proteins to bind calcium.
4. Vitamin K deficiency is common in healthy adults.
5. There is no dietary upper limit for vitamin K.
6. Thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. **True.** K2 menaquinone is synthesised by intestinal bacteria. (§1, §2)
2. **False.** It is fat-soluble. K1 is absorbed in micelles and both forms leave the liver in VLDL. (§2)
3. **True.** That is the point of carboxylation. (§3)
4. **False.** Rare in adults. Newborns are the risk group. (§7)
5. **True.** No evidence of toxicity from natural forms. (§7)
6. **True.** Then fibrin forms the clot. (§4)

</details>
