## Part A: Core questions

### Q1: Sources and requirement

Name five good iodine sources, state the EFSA AI for adults and pregnancy, and explain why the iodine content of foods varies so much.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: sources**

Marine fish, shellfish, seaweed, milk and dairy, eggs. Iodised salt where available.

**Step 2: AI**

150 µg/day for adults, about 200 µg/day in pregnancy.

**Step 3: variation**

Iodine content depends on the soil, the water, the feed given to animals, and processing. It is a trace element, so it follows the soil.

### Q2: The thyroid loop

Name the two thyroid hormones and say how many iodine atoms each has. Then describe the control loop from the hypothalamus to the thyroid, including the feedback.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: hormones**

Thyroxine (T4), four iodine atoms. Triiodothyronine (T3), three.

**Step 2: loop**

Hypothalamus releases TRH. TRH makes the anterior pituitary release TSH. TSH makes the thyroid release T3 and T4.

**Step 3: feedback**

T3 and T4 feed back to switch off TRH and TSH. Negative feedback.

From: §3

</details>

### Q4: Goitre and cretinism

Explain how goitre develops from iodine deficiency. Then describe cretinism: who gets it, the features, and whether it can be treated.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: goitre**

Low iodine means low T3 and T4. There is no negative feedback, so TSH stays high. TSH keeps stimulating the thyroid, which enlarges. A large goitre can obstruct the trachea.

**Step 2: cretinism**

A baby born to an iodine-deficient mother. Severe mental retardation, poor growth, increased risk of death. It cannot be reversed. Iodine deficiency is the most common cause of preventable mental impairment worldwide.

From: §4, §2

</details>

### Q5: Ireland and the UK

Summarise what the slides say about iodine status in UK schoolgirls, Irish pregnant women and Irish adults. What is the main Irish source and how does status change with season?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: UK schoolgirls**

51% mild deficiency, 16% moderate, 1% severe, measured by urinary iodine. Linked to summer sampling, low milk and high egg intake.

**Step 2: Irish pregnant women**

Almost one in three moderately deficient. Less than 5% of salt in Ireland is iodised.

**Step 3: Irish adults**

55% below the AI of 150 µg; 64.7% of women. Almost nobody above the UL.

**Step 4: source and season**

Milk. Intake and urinary iodine rise with milk consumption. Status is highest January to March and lowest July to September.

From: §2

</details>

### Q6: Intervention, hypo and hyper

What is the public health fix for iodine deficiency and how widespread is it? Compare hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism in terms of TSH, T4/T3 and symptoms.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: fix**

Iodised salt. Simple, effective, cheap. UNICEF says 66% of households have access. Mild deficiency persists where it is not mandatory, which is a concern in pregnancy.

**Step 2: hypothyroidism**

High TSH, low T4 and T3. Low basal metabolic rate, slow reflex relaxation, slow movements, cold intolerance.

**Step 3: hyperthyroidism**

Excess T4 and T3. Hyperactivity, weight loss, difficulty sleeping.

From: §5, §6

</details>
