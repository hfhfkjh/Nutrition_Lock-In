## Part A: Core questions

### Q1: Sources and numbers

Name the richest selenium food and its content. Give the EFSA AI, the lactation AI, the RDA and the UL. What kind of foods supply selenium in general?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: richest**

Brazil nuts, 544 µg per ounce (6–8 nuts).

**Step 2: numbers**

EFSA AI 70 µg/day, 85 µg lactating. RDA 55 µg/day. UL 400 µg/day.

**Step 3: in general**

High-protein foods: seafood, meat, poultry, eggs, dairy, whole grains.

</details>

### Q2: Metabolism

Describe selenium's dietary forms, absorption, conversion, storage and excretion.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: forms**

Organic: selenomethionine and selenocysteine. Inorganic: selenite and selenate.

**Step 2: absorption**

Mainly in the small intestine, generally efficient, bioavailability depends on the form.

**Step 3: conversion**

All forms are converted to selenide, which is used to make selenoproteins.

**Step 4: storage and excretion**

Selenomethionine can be incorporated into body proteins. Excretion is mainly in urine.

From: §2

</details>

### Q3: Function

Explain selenium's antioxidant role, naming the enzyme and the vitamin it works with. What two other things does it support? What is the evidence link with chronic disease?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: antioxidant**

Selenium is in glutathione peroxidase, which destroys peroxides. It works closely with vitamin E, which catches free radicals in membranes.

**Step 2: other roles**

Thyroid function and vitamin C metabolism. Immune function.

**Step 3: chronic disease**

Higher selenium status is associated with lower risk of cancer, cardiovascular disease, Alzheimer's disease and thyroid disorders. Association, not proof.

From: §3

</details>

---

### Q4: Deficiency and toxicity

Name and describe the two selenium deficiency diseases. How does selenium deficiency interact with iodine? List the signs of toxicity and the UL.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: diseases**

Keshan disease: cardiomyopathy, in women and children. Kashin-Beck disease: osteoarthritis in children. Both from selenium-poor regions. Deficiency rarely causes illness otherwise.

**Step 2: iodine**

Selenium deficiency can worsen iodine deficiency and increase the risk of hypothyroidism, because selenium enzymes are needed for thyroid hormone metabolism.

**Step 3: toxicity**

UL 400 µg/day. Hair and nail loss, garlic odour, metallic taste, skin rash, nausea, diarrhoea, fatigue, irritability, nervous system abnormalities.

From: §4

</details>
