## Part A: Core questions

### Q1: Forms of vitamin A

Name the three preformed forms of vitamin A and say which chemical group each one has.

Then name the main pro-vitamin A and say what it is cleaved into.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: preformed forms**

| Form | Group |
|---|---|
| Retinol | alcohol (–CH₂OH) |
| Retinal | aldehyde (–CHO) |
| Retinoic acid | acid (–COOH) |

**Step 2: pro-vitamin**

β-carotene (also α, γ-carotene and cryptoxanthin).

It is cleaved in the gut by carotene dioxygenase to give retinaldehyde (retinal).

### Q2: RAE calculation

A serving of mixed vegetables contains 1200 µg β-carotene and 480 µg of other carotenes. It contains no retinol.

How many µg RAE is that? What fraction of a woman's RDA does it cover?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: convert each carotene**

β-carotene: 1200 ÷ 12 = 100 µg RAE

Other carotenes: 480 ÷ 24 = 20 µg RAE

**Step 2: add up**

Total = **120 µg RAE**

**Step 3: compare with the RDA**

Woman's RDA = 600 µg RAE.

120 ÷ 600 = **0.2**, so one fifth of the RDA.

From: §2, Equation 1, §3

</details>

### Q3: Why carotenes count for less

Give three reasons why β-carotene has lower vitamin A activity than retinol.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Any three of:

- Poor absorption (5–60%, against 70–90% for retinol).
- Poor activity of carotene dioxygenase in the gut.
- Other carotenoids can inhibit carotene dioxygenase.
- Cleavage sometimes happens at the wrong place, giving apo-carotenes that become retinoic acid, not retinol.

From: §2

</details>

### Q4: Absorption and transport

Describe the journey of dietary retinol from food to the liver, and how the liver releases it into the blood.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: in the gut**

Retinol esters in food are hydrolysed by lipases.

**Step 2: into the lymph**

In the gut wall the retinol is re-esterified and packed into chylomicrons. These go into the lymph, then the blood.

**Step 3: in the liver**

Chylomicrons deliver vitamin A to the liver. It is stored there in lipid droplets.

**Step 4: out again**

When needed, the ester is hydrolysed and retinol is released bound to retinol binding protein (RBP) and transthyretin.

From: §4

</details>

### Q5: The visual cycle

Explain how vitamin A takes part in vision, and why deficiency causes night blindness.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: rhodopsin**

Rod cells in the retina contain rhodopsin = opsin (protein) + 11-cis-retinal (vitamin A).

**Step 2: light**

Light converts cis-retinal to trans-retinal. Opsin and trans-retinal split. That split sends the nerve signal to the brain.

**Step 3: recycling**

trans-retinal is converted back to cis-retinal and rhodopsin reforms. Some is lost as retinoic acid, so fresh vitamin A is needed.

**Step 4: deficiency**

With no spare retinal, rhodopsin cannot be rebuilt fast enough. Dark adaptation slows and vision in dim light fails. That is night blindness.

From: §5, §7

</details>

### Q6: Gene expression

Describe, in numbered steps, how retinoic acid changes gene expression. Name the receptors and the DNA element involved.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

1. Retinoic acid moves into the nucleus.
2. It binds to its receptors: RAR (binds all-trans retinoic acid) and RXR (binds 9-cis retinoic acid). These sit on the retinoic acid response element (RARE) in the promoter of certain genes.
3. Binding enhances transcription of those genes.
4. The effect depends on the tissue. In epithelial cells it drives cell differentiation.

Genes affected encode structural proteins (keratins), enzymes (alcohol dehydrogenase), and proteins for cell differentiation and immune cells.

From: §6

</details>

### Q7: Deficiency, status and toxicity

a. List the stages of eye disease in vitamin A deficiency, in order.

b. Name two ways to assess vitamin A status.

c. What is the main risk of too much vitamin A in pregnancy, and what is the UL?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**a.** Night blindness → xerophthalmia (dry eye, Bitot's spots) → irreversible blindness.

**b.** Any two of: liver biopsy (direct, invasive), dark adaptation time, Bitot's spots, relative dose response test (% change in serum after a dose).

**c.** It is teratogenic (birth defects). UL for pregnant women = 3000–3300 µg per day.

From: §7, §8, §9

</details>
