## Part A: Core questions

### Q1: Where and how much

How much calcium is in the adult body? Split it into its two pools and say what each pool is for.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: amount**

About 1200 g. It is the most abundant mineral in the body.

**Step 2: the pools**

99% in bones and teeth: structure, and a calcium bank.

1% in body fluids (blood, extracellular fluid, muscle): muscle contraction, nerve transmission, blood clotting, vascular tone.

From: §1, §6

</details>

### Q2: Portions to the RDA

A 16-year-old wants to meet their calcium RDA from yoghurt alone. One small carton gives about 200 mg.

1. How many cartons would they need in a day?
2. How many would an adult need?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: the RDAs**

11–17 years: 1200 mg. Adults: 800 mg.

**Step 2: divide**

1200 ÷ 200 = **6** cartons for the teenager.

800 ÷ 200 = **4** cartons for the adult.

### Q3: Absorption

Of 1000 mg calcium eaten, how much is absorbed? Describe the two absorption routes and name two dietary factors that reduce absorption.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: amount**

About 300 mg, 30%.

**Step 2: routes**

Active transport: vitamin D dependent, uses calbindin, about 20–30%.

Passive transport: concentration dependent, between cells, about 50–70%.

**Step 3: dietary blockers**

Oxalates and phytates in foods.

From: §3

</details>

### Q4: The feedback loop

Blood calcium falls. Name the hormone released and the three organs it acts on. Say what happens at each organ. Then do the same for a rise in blood calcium.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: calcium falls**

PTH is released.

Kidney: PTH + vitamin D increase reabsorption, less calcium in urine.

Intestine: PTH increases vitamin D activation, vitamin D increases absorption.

Bone: PTH + vitamin D increase resorption, calcium leaves bone.

Blood calcium rises, which switches PTH off.

**Step 2: calcium rises**

Calcitonin is released.

Kidney: reabsorption prevented. Intestine: absorption limited. Bone: resorption inhibited. Calcitonin also inhibits vitamin D.

Blood calcium falls, which switches calcitonin off.

From: §5

</details>

---

### Q5: Vitamin D and calbindin

Explain how PTH changes vitamin D metabolism, and what calcitriol does in the gut wall.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: PTH and the kidney**

PTH is secreted when calcium is low. It increases the action of 1-hydroxylase in the kidney, so more 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol) is made.

**Step 2: calcitriol in the gut**

Calcitriol up-regulates calbindin, the calcium transport protein. More calbindin means more active absorption.

Calcitriol also inhibits its own synthesis and increases 24-hydroxylase, which makes the inactive 24-hydroxy form.

From: §4

</details>

### Q6: Bone through life

Describe bone turnover at three life stages. What is peak bone mass and why does it matter?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: three stages**

Growth: formation exceeds breakdown.

Early and middle adulthood: about equal.

Older adult: breakdown exceeds formation, about 150 mg per day lost.

**Step 2: peak bone mass**

The highest bone mass reached, in the teens and twenties. Adequate calcium intake is essential for it. A higher peak means more bone to lose before reaching the level linked with fractures.

From: §7

</details>

---

### Q7: Status, deficiency, toxicity

How is calcium status measured? Give the WHO definition of osteoporosis. List three toxicity effects and the US upper limit.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: status**

DEXA scan, measuring bone mineral content and bone mineral density (BMC per area).

**Step 2: osteoporosis**

BMD more than 2.5 SD below the young adult mean. Osteopenia is −1 to −2.5 SD.

**Step 3: toxicity**

UL 2500 mg/day. Kidney stones, calcification of soft tissue, reduced absorption of iron and zinc. Linked to supplement use.

From: §8, §9

</details>
