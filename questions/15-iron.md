## Part A: Core questions

### Q1: Haem versus non-haem

Compare haem and non-haem iron: where each is found, what share of intake each gives, how well each is absorbed, and which is affected by other foods.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: where**

Haem: only animal foods (meat, poultry, fish), about 40% of the iron in meat. Non-haem: plant foods and the other 60% of meat iron.

**Step 2: share of intake**

Haem about 10%, non-haem about 90%.

**Step 3: absorption**

Haem 15–30%, absorbed intact via HCP1. Non-haem 1–20%, absorbed as Fe²⁺ via DMT1.

**Step 4: dietary factors**

Non-haem is strongly influenced by other dietary components. Haem is not much affected.

From: §2, §4

</details>

### Q2: Portions to the PRI

A woman needs 16 mg iron a day (EFSA PRI). Lean beef gives 3.1 mg per 120 g portion. Spinach gives 1.8 mg per 90 g.

1. How many portions of beef would reach the PRI?
2. How many portions of spinach?
3. Why is the spinach answer misleading even once you have it?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: beef**

16 ÷ 3.1 = 5.2, so **6** portions.

**Step 2: spinach**

16 ÷ 1.8 = 8.9, so **9** portions.

**Step 3: why misleading**

Spinach iron is non-haem, absorbed at only 1–20%, and spinach contains oxalates. Beef iron is partly haem, absorbed at 15–30%, and meat also boosts non-haem absorption from the rest of the meal.

From: §2, Equation 1, §5

</details>

### Q3: Iron in the body

How much iron is in a 70 kg adult? Split it into pools, naming the protein for each. Where is excess stored?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: amount**

4 to 5 g.

**Step 2: pools**

70% functional: haemoglobin 60%, myoglobin 5%, enzymes 5%.

20% ferritin (storage). 10% haemosiderin (storage). Less than 1% transferrin (transport).

**Step 3: excess**

Liver, bone marrow and spleen.

### Q4: Absorption and hepcidin

Describe how non-haem iron is absorbed, step by step, and explain how hepcidin changes it in deficiency and in overload.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: the steps**

Fe³⁺ is reduced to Fe²⁺. Fe²⁺ crosses the brush border via DMT1. Inside the gut cell it binds ferritin as a temporary store. It enters the blood as needed and binds transferrin. Iron not needed is lost when the cell is shed.

**Step 2: hepcidin**

Hepcidin, from the liver, regulates synthesis of the transport proteins.

Deficiency: low hepcidin, more transport proteins, more absorption.

Overload: high hepcidin, fewer transport proteins, less absorption, iron-rich cells shed in faeces.

From: §4

</details>

### Q5: Helpers and blockers

List the dietary factors that increase and decrease non-haem iron absorption. Explain the vitamin C mechanism and give the tea figure.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: increase**

Vitamin C, meat and fish. Also depleted iron status and pregnancy.

**Step 2: decrease**

Phytate (legumes, whole grains), polyphenols (tea), calcium, low gastric acid, replete iron status.

**Step 3: vitamin C**

Donates an electron to ferric Fe³⁺, reducing it to ferrous Fe²⁺, the form that is absorbed.

**Step 4: tea**

A cup of tea with a meal reduces non-haem iron absorption by 40–70%.

From: §5

</details>

### Q6: Balance and functions

Why can the body not simply excrete excess iron? What three things does it do instead? List six functions of iron with the protein involved.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: no excretion**

There is no physiological mechanism for excreting iron.

**Step 2: instead**

Store it (ferritin), reuse it, regulate absorption.

**Step 3: functions**

Oxygen transport (haemoglobin). Oxygen storage in muscle (myoglobin). ATP via the electron transport chain (cytochromes). Cofactor for non-haem energy enzymes. Cofactor for antioxidant enzymes. Cofactor for DNA synthesis.

From: §6, §7

</details>

### Q7: Deficiency

Who is most at risk of iron deficiency and why? List the signs. Describe the three stages and what is measured at each. Give the WHO haemoglobin cut-offs.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: at risk**

Infants, adolescents and pregnant women, because requirements are high during growth. Women of reproductive age because of menstrual losses.

**Step 2: signs**

Fatigue, weakness, poor resistance to cold, pale skin, reduced intellectual ability in children. Anaemia when haemoglobin is too low.

**Step 3: stages**

1. Stores depleted: serum ferritin below 12 µg/L.
2. Transport iron falls: serum iron down, iron-binding capacity up.
3. Haemoglobin production limited: free protoporphyrin up, haemoglobin down.

**Step 4: WHO**

Hb below 13 g/dl in men, below 12 g/dl in women.

From: §9

</details>

1. Ferric iron (Fe³⁺) is the form absorbed in the gut.
