## Part A: Core questions

### Q1: Structure and names

Name the three parts of the folate molecule.

What is the difference between folate and folic acid? What does "80% of dietary folates are polyglutamates" mean?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: the three parts**

Pteridine, PABA (para-aminobenzoic acid), glutamic acid.

**Step 2: the two names**

Folate is the natural form in food. Folic acid is the synthetic form in supplements and fortified foods.

**Step 3: polyglutamates**

Most food folate has a chain of several glutamic acids, not just one. These extra glutamates must be cut off by conjugase before absorption.

From: §1, §4

</details>

### Q3: Dietary folate equivalents

A day's food contains 180 µg of natural folate from vegetables and legumes.
It also contains a bowl of fortified cereal with 100 µg of synthetic folic acid.

Work out the total in µg DFE. Does it meet the adult PRI of 330 µg?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: food folate**

Food folate counts 1 for 1.

180 µg food folate = 180 µg DFE

**Step 2: folic acid**

Folic acid counts 1.7 times, because it is about 100% available while food folate is about 50%.

100 × 1.7 = 170 µg DFE

**Step 3: total**

180 + 170 = **350 µg DFE**

**Step 4: compare**

350 is above 330, so yes, it meets the adult PRI.

It would not meet the pregnancy PRI of 600 µg.

From: §3, Equation 1

</details>

### Q4: Absorption and activation

Describe how dietary folate is absorbed and converted into the form that circulates in the blood. Name the enzyme and the mineral it needs.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: hydrolysis**

In the small intestine, conjugase cuts the extra glutamates off polyglutamate folates. Conjugase is zinc-dependent.

**Step 2: absorption**

Folate is absorbed by active transport.

**Step 3: reduction**

In the intestinal cell, a reductase using NADPH adds two hydrogens to make dihydrofolate (DHF), then two more to make tetrahydrofolate (THF). Four hydrogens in total.

**Step 4: methylation**

THF is methylated to 5-methyl-THF, which enters the bloodstream and goes to the liver, then the rest of the body.

From: §4

</details>

### Q5: Single-carbon transfers

What is the job of THF in metabolism? Name the four one-carbon units and the folate form that carries each.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: the job**

THF carries single-carbon units from one reaction to another. This is needed for amino acid metabolism, DNA and RNA synthesis, and neurotransmitters.

**Step 2: the forms**

| Folate form | Unit |
|---|---|
| 5-methyl-THF | methyl, CH₃ |
| 5,10-methylene-THF | methylene, CH₂ |
| 5,10-methenyl-THF | methenyl, CH= |
| 10-formyl-THF | formyl, O=CH |

From: §5

</details>

### Q6: DNA and methionine

Explain the two ways folate is needed for DNA. In the second, say which other vitamin is involved.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: nucleotides**

Folate is needed to make adenine, guanine and thymidine. 5,10-methylene-THF converts dUMP to dTMP (thymidylate), which goes into DNA.

**Step 2: methionine**

Folate is needed to make methionine, which is required for DNA methylation (gene expression). 5-methyl-THF passes its methyl group to vitamin B12, which passes it to homocysteine to make methionine.

From: §6

</details>

---

### Q7: Megaloblastic anaemia

Explain why folate deficiency causes megaloblastic anaemia. Which other tissues are affected, and why?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: the cause**

Red blood cells divide rapidly and need lots of thymidine for DNA. Without folate, DNA strands break and cell division slows, but RNA synthesis continues, so the cells grow large and keep their nucleus.

**Step 2: the result**

Large, irregular, macrocytic red cells. Megaloblastic anaemia.

**Step 3: other tissues**

Bone marrow, intestine and hair follicles. All of these divide rapidly, so they also need lots of thymidine.

From: §7

</details>

### Q8: Pregnancy and public health

a. Name the two neural tube defects on the slides.

b. What should every woman of childbearing age take, how much, and when?

c. What is different for a woman with a previous NTD pregnancy?

d. What did the FSAI propose in 2016 as Option 1?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**a.** Anencephaly (upper neural tube fails to close, brain does not develop) and spina bifida (incomplete closure of the spinal cord).

**b.** A daily 400 µg folic acid supplement, before conception and for the first 12 weeks of pregnancy.

**c.** 4 mg (4000 µg) daily, for the first 4 months of pregnancy and at least 1 month before conception.

**d.** Mandatory fortification of bread or flour to give about 150 µg folic acid per day to women of childbearing age, which could cut NTDs by about 30%. Plus advice to take an extra 400 µg daily. Voluntary fortification would continue.

From: §8, §9

</details>
