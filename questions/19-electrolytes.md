### Q2: Sodium from salt

The Irish salt target for adults is 6 g a day. A packet of crisps contains 0.5 g salt and a ready meal contains 2.5 g salt.

1. How much sodium is in the target?
2. How much sodium is in the crisps and the ready meal together?
3. A food label says 1.2 g sodium. How much salt is that?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: the rule**

Sodium = 0.4 × salt. Salt = sodium ÷ 0.4.

**Step 2: the target**

6 × 0.4 = **2.4 g sodium**.

**Step 3: the foods**

0.5 + 2.5 = 3 g salt. 3 × 0.4 = **1.2 g sodium**.

**Step 4: label**

1.2 ÷ 0.4 = **3 g salt**.

From: §3, Equation 1

</details>

### Q3: The pump

Describe the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump: what it moves, what it costs, what share of resting energy it uses, and what it is needed for. Give the inside and outside concentrations of sodium and potassium.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: what it moves**

3 Na⁺ out of the cell and 2 K⁺ into the cell per cycle, against their concentration gradients.

**Step 2: cost**

One ATP per cycle. 20–40% of resting energy expenditure.

**Step 3: needed for**

Nerve impulse transmission, muscle contraction, cardiac function. It restores the gradients after sodium and potassium switch places during an impulse.

**Step 4: concentrations**

Na⁺: 10 mM inside, 142 mM outside. K⁺: 150 mM inside, 5 mM outside.

From: §2

</details>

### Q4: Fluid balance and functions

Explain how the body uses sodium to control where water goes. Then list the functions of sodium and the function of chloride.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: water follows sodium**

Ions are moved by active transport. Water moves passively to where sodium and chloride are high. If blood sodium falls, water moves into the tissues. The kidney uses sodium to retain water.

**Step 2: sodium**

Nerve conduction and muscle contraction, active cellular transport via the Na/K pump, formation of bone apatite (75% of body sodium is in bone).

**Step 3: chloride**

Production of HCl in the stomach.

From: §4, §5

</details>

### Q5: Aldosterone and RAAS

What happens to aldosterone and urinary sodium when blood sodium is high, and when it is low? Describe the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system step by step, naming where each part is made.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: aldosterone**

High blood sodium: aldosterone falls, sodium is excreted in urine. Low blood sodium: aldosterone rises, kidney retains sodium.

**Step 2: RAAS**

Renin, an enzyme from the kidney, converts angiotensinogen (from the liver) to angiotensin I. Angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE), in the lungs, converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II. Angiotensin II constricts small arteries and stimulates aldosterone release. Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption.

From: §6, §7

</details>

### Q6: Potassium and deficiency

Summarise potassium: location, absorption, regulation, aldosterone's effect, intake versus optimal, sources. Then describe when electrolyte deficiency happens and its symptoms.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Step 1: potassium**

Major intracellular electrolyte. 90% absorbed. Kidney regulates excretion in urine. Aldosterone has the opposite effect to sodium: keeps NaCl, loses K. We eat about 2 g a day but 3.5 g is optimal. Sources: legumes, nuts, meat, fish, bananas, spinach, potatoes.

**Step 2: deficiency**

Rare. Extreme sport with excessive sweating and no electrolyte replacement, or drinking too much water without electrolytes. Nausea, dizziness, muscle cramps, irritability, confusion, low blood pressure.

From: §8, §9

</details>
